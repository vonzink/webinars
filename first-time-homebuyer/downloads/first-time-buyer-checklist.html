First Time Buyer Checklist

The nine stages, what happens at each one, and the eight things that can undo your approval.
From Page Two — What Nobody Explains About Buying Your First Home

The 9 Stages

1
Financial readiness. A conversation, not an application. Credit pulled, income and assets reviewed. Nothing is committed. "No" is almost never the answer — "not yet, and here's what changes it" is.
2
Pre-approval. Credit pulled, documents collected. If you'll be competing, ask by name: "Can you get me underwritten up front?"
3
House hunting. Third — not first. With a known budget and a letter in hand.
4
Offer & contract. Earnest money deposited. Contingency deadlines begin — these are contractual dates, not suggestions.
5
Loan application. Your Loan Estimate must arrive within 3 business days.
6
Processing. Documents, verifications, appraisal ordered.
7
Underwriting → conditions. The anxious part. You'll be asked for things you already sent. Send them again, same day. It's a checklist, not suspicion.
8
Clear to close. Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days before you sign. Compare it to your Loan Estimate, line by line.
9
Closing. Sign, fund, record, keys.

When you're on day 14 and it feels like nothing is happening — it's because you're in underwriting, which looks exactly like nothing happening.

Before You Start — 7 Things To Do

  • Pull your credit. Free annual report from the official federal site (annualcreditreport.com). Look for errors — fix them early, not during your loan.
  • Run the Payment-Shock Test. For 3 months, transfer the difference between your rent and your projected all-in payment into savings. You find out if it's livable — and you end up with the money.
  • Write down your Number. The highest payment you could make every month for ten years and still take the trip, still fix the car.
  • Call one lender. Not to apply. Twenty minutes.
  • List your debts — balance, minimum payment, rate. That's your DTI numerator.
  • Ask about assistance. Have someone run all four layers for your county and income: state (CHFA), metro/city, county, and employer/profession.
  • If you or your spouse served — get your Certificate of Eligibility. Even if you think it doesn't count.

The Do-Not-Do List

From application to keys. Your approval stays alive — and killable — until you have the keys. Lenders re-pull credit before closing. Every time.

  • No new credit. Cards, cars, furniture, phones, buy-now-pay-later. Even at 0%.
  • Don't close existing accounts. It shortens your history and raises utilization.
  • No large purchases on cards you already have.
  • No job changes — especially W-2 to self-employed, or salary to commission.
  • Don't move money between accounts without a clear paper trail.
  • Don't accept a cash gift without telling your lender first. Gift funds have a documentation process; undocumented deposits become a problem.
  • Don't co-sign anything for anyone.
  • Don't miss a payment on anything.

The rule that covers all eight: from application to keys, your financial life is a museum exhibit. Look, don't touch.

The escape valve: if something has to change — a job offer, an inheritance, a car that died — that's fine. Life happens. Call your loan officer first. Almost every one of these is survivable with warning and fatal as a surprise.

What Kills Deals After Contract

WhatWhy it matters
New debtChanges your ratios after approval. The single most common preventable failure.
Job changeIncome stability has to be re-established. Self-employment resets the clock.
Unexplained depositsEvery dollar has to have a documented story.
Low appraisalThe gap has to come from somewhere — a price cut, your cash, or a renegotiation.
Missed deadlinesInspection and financing dates are contractual. Earnest money can be at risk.

Questions To Ask Everyone

  • "Who pays you, and how?" — ask your agent, your loan officer, everyone. Nobody good is offended by it.
  • "Can you get me underwritten up front?" — to your lender, if you'll be competing.
  • "What's your Section A?" — to every lender you talk to.
  • "Do you offer a float-down if rates improve after I lock?" — ask before you lock.
  • "Can I have a lender credit instead of paying points?" — if cash is tight.

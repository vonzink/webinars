# Transitions

**Transitions are content, not connective tissue.** A deck where every transition is
"so, next..." reads as a list. A deck where each transition creates the next
question reads as an argument.

Every transition below does one of three jobs:
- **Question** — plants the question the next module answers
- **Consequence** — the next module is the *result* of what just happened
- **Turn** — deliberately reverses direction

---

## Module transitions

| From → To | Type | Line |
|---|---|---|
| **1 → 2** | Question | *"So let's start where every one of you is going to start, whether you know it or not. Not with a house. With a phone call you're dreading."* |
| **2 → 3** | Turn | *"Okay. You know the road now. Which brings us to the thing that actually keeps people up at night — and it isn't the process. It's the number."* |
| **3 → 4** | Consequence | *"So now you know your number. Which raises the obvious next problem — and it's the one that stops more first-time buyers than anything else on this list. Not the payment. The pile of cash you think you need before anyone will even talk to you."* |
| **4 → 5** | Question | *"So the down payment is smaller than you thought. Good. Now let's talk about the number everybody obsesses over and almost nobody understands — and I'm going to show you why the lowest rate is not the cheapest loan."* |
| **5 → 6** | Consequence | *"So you know how rates work, what points cost, and where the money goes. All of that is theory until somebody hands you a piece of paper with actual numbers on it. That paper has a name, it is legally standardized, and it is the single most powerful consumer-protection document in this entire process — and I'd bet most people reading it have never made it to page two."* |
| **6 → 7** | Question | *"Which is the perfect handoff. Because the thing that determines what's in Section A in the first place — the thing that sets your pricing before any negotiating happens — is a three-digit number you probably think you already know. You probably don't."* |
| **7 → 8** | Turn | *"That's your side of the equation. Let's talk about the other side — because where you buy changes the math more than most first-time buyers realize, and there's a specific offer you're very likely to encounter that deserves a closer look than it usually gets."* |
| **8 → 9** | Consequence | *"Alright. Minute thirty-six. About four minutes left of me talking, and I want to spend it on the most expensive part of this entire webinar. Not the most complicated. The most expensive."* |
| **9 → 10** | Turn | *"Which brings us to the last two minutes. And I want to use them to make sure you leave with something in your hands — not just something in your head."* |

## Story entries and exits

Stories need a **cold entry** (no wind-up) and a **hard exit** (state the lesson once,
then move). Wind-up telegraphs that a story is coming and drains it.

| Story | Entry | Exit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Reyes | *"Marcus and Dani spent five months looking."* | *"They lost it because they did step four before step one."* |
| 2 — Priya | *"Priya came to me pre-approved for five hundred twenty thousand."* | *"That's mistake number seven. So you're going to ask the other question. Right now."* |
| 3 — Tom & Ashley | *"Tom and Ashley decided they'd do it right."* | *"But it should be a decision, not a default."* |
| 4 — VA | *"A few years back I'm on a first call with a guy."* | *"Ask anyway. He almost paid tens of thousands of dollars for the privilege of not asking a question."* |
| 5 — Two LEs | *"A client sent me two Loan Estimates and asked which was better."* | *"The disclosure only works if somebody reads page two."* |
| 6 — $4,100 short | *"A buyer told me, very confidently, that his closing costs were fifty-four hundred dollars."* | *"Those are not the same word, and they are twenty-six thousand dollars apart."* |
| 7 — The sofa | *"Nine days before closing, a client bought furniture."* | *"Nobody told him the approval was still alive."* |
| 8 — Builder | *"Client's under contract on a new build."* | *"Sometimes the builder's lender is the right answer."* |

## Micro-transitions worth keeping

These are small and they do real work:

- **Into a demo:** *"Let me just show you."* — never *"Now I'm going to demonstrate."*
- **Out of a demo:** name the takeaway in one sentence, then move. Don't recap the
  clicks.
- **Into a poll:** *"Quick one."* Always. It signals low cost and raises participation.
- **After a poll reveal:** *"So — a chunk of this room just told me..."* — never
  *"a lot of you got that wrong."*
- **Into an exercise:** *"Everybody, right now."* Present tense, imperative, no
  preamble.
- **Seeding a mistake:** *"That's mistake number four — hold onto that."* Say the
  number. It's what makes the recap function as retrieval rather than repetition.
- **Before a hard truth:** *"And I want to be careful here."* or *"Here's the
  uncomfortable part, and I'm including myself in this."*
- **Before an anti-urgency statement:** *"Now — the honest counterweight, because I
  told you I wouldn't sell you anything."*

## Transitions to avoid

| Don't say | Why |
|---|---|
| "So, moving on..." | Signals the last section didn't matter |
| "As you can see..." | They may not. Say what they should see. |
| "I know this is boring, but..." | Never apologize for content you chose to include. Cut it or commit to it. |
| "We're running short on time..." | Audience anxiety. Use a `[CUT-n]` silently instead. |
| "Does that make sense?" | Rhetorical, unanswerable at scale, and it invites doubt. Ask a specific question or move. |
| "Long story short..." | You already told the long story. |
| "This is really important." | Everything you kept should be. Show importance with pacing, not labels. |

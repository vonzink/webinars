## MODULE 2 — THE DOWN PAYMENT LIE (11:10–16:45) ⏱

**[018]** 💬 Poll. How much do you think you need down on a four-fifty house?

*(20 seconds. 🔴 **Hold the results. Say nothing.**)*

**[019]** So — a chunk of this room just told me they need ninety thousand dollars.

*(beat)*

The real number, for most of you, is somewhere between **zero and fifteen thousand
seven hundred fifty.**

VA — zero down. USDA — zero down. Conventional — three percent, thirteen five. FHA —
three and a half, fifteen seven fifty.

Where did twenty percent come from? It's not made up. **At twenty percent down you
avoid mortgage insurance.** That's a real benefit and that's the origin.

Somewhere along the way "avoids one specific fee" became "required." And that
translation has cost a generation of buyers more than it ever saved them.

**[020]** Ashley kept the number on a Post-it on the bathroom mirror. **Seventy-one
thousand dollars.** Twenty percent of the house they wanted, on the street they
wanted, in 2019.

They were serious. No vacations that mattered. Tom updated a spreadsheet every
payday.

Four years in they had **fifty-eight thousand dollars** — which is genuinely
extraordinary, and I want to be clear that I admire it.

And one morning Ashley came out of the bathroom and she'd changed the Post-it. Same
house. Same street. New number.

**Ninety-four thousand.**

*(beat)*

She told me that was the morning she cried, and that she didn't think it was about
the money. It was that she'd done everything right and the finish line moved anyway.

They spent six years chasing a percentage of a number that kept moving — to avoid a
fee that would have come off in five.

And the honest other side, because I told you I wouldn't sell you anything: if
they'd bought right before a downturn, this story reads differently. **Waiting isn't
always wrong. But it should be a decision, not a default.** Theirs was a default.
Nobody ever told them there was a comparison to run.

**[021]** Four ways in. Quickly.

**Conventional, three percent down.** And "first-time buyer" doesn't mean what you
think — most programs define it as no ownership interest in a primary residence in
the last **three years.** If you owned something a decade ago, you're probably a
first-time buyer again.

There are two versions worth asking for by name — **HomeReady** and **Home
Possible.** Income limits apply, but they carry reduced mortgage insurance and
better pricing at the same loan-to-value. If you're eligible they're frequently the
best product available to a first-time buyer, and they are chronically
under-mentioned.

**FHA, three and a half percent.** More forgiving on credit, higher DTI tolerance.
Genuinely great program. One tradeoff nobody mentions at the sales table: on most FHA
loans with less than ten percent down, **the mortgage insurance lasts the life of the
loan.** Conventional's comes off. FHA's usually doesn't. That doesn't make it wrong —
it makes it a choice you should make on purpose.

**USDA, zero down** — if the property's in an eligible area and you're under the
household income limit. "Rural" doesn't mean a farm; plenty of normal Colorado towns
qualify. It's a map. Check the address, not the assumption.

**And VA.** A few years back I'm on a first call with a guy. Good job, decent credit,
scraping together three and a half percent for FHA. Halfway through, completely
offhand, he mentions he did four years in the Army after high school.

I stopped the call. I said — you know you can buy this with nothing down? And no
monthly mortgage insurance at all?

He didn't. **He was thirty-four. Nobody had ever asked him.**

Zero down, no monthly mortgage insurance, and it qualifies on **residual income** —
what's actually left after everything — which is a more humane test than a ratio.
It's reusable, not once per lifetime. Active duty, veterans, Guard, Reserves, some
surviving spouses.

And if you're thinking "it was only a few years, it probably doesn't count" — **ask
anyway.** That's the lesson. He almost paid tens of thousands of dollars for the
privilege of not asking a question.

**[022]** Now the part you can't find on a national website. Colorado has four layers
of down payment assistance and almost nobody checks all four.

**CHFA** — the state agency. A first mortgage plus assistance, as a grant or a
second. Income and price limits by county. Participating lenders only.

**Metro and city.** Several Front Range municipalities run their own.

**County.** Varies enormously. Worth ten minutes.

**And employer or profession** — teachers, first responders, healthcare, large
employers, unions, credit unions. **Nobody ever asks their HR department whether
their employer has a housing benefit. Some of you have one. It costs you one email.**

Here's my obligation to you: **these programs change, and some run out of money
mid-year.** So the takeaway isn't a list. It's the instruction — **ask your lender to
run all four layers for your county and your income, on the day you're actually
shopping.**

**[023]** Last thing here. Mortgage insurance is a phase, not a sentence.

On conventional, you can **request removal at eighty percent** loan-to-value — you
have to ask, nobody calls you — and it **terminates automatically at seventy-eight**
by the original schedule. There's an appreciation-based route too, but servicer rules
vary, so get their policy in writing.

On this loan that's sixty-seven thousand five hundred of paydown. **That hundred
twenty-five a month is not forever.**

And the decision tree, five questions. Did you or your spouse serve? VA — check it
first. USDA-eligible area and under the income limit? USDA. Credit under about
six-sixty? FHA is likely. Under the HomeReady limit? Probably that. None of the
above? Conventional.

**Then layer assistance on top.** It isn't a program you pick instead. It's a floor
you stack under the one you picked.

**[029]** One more thing on that page. Prepaids and escrows get lumped in with

And a real tactic: interest on this loan is about **seventy-six dollars a day.**

**No.** Mortgage inquiries inside a shopping window — commonly fourteen to forty-five

Getting three Loan Estimates costs you essentially nothing. Not getting them cost
that woman six thousand four hundred dollars.

**[031]** So — sixty seconds, any two lenders.

Same day. Same loan amount, program, and lock period. **Compare Section A.** Note the

*(beat, slow)*

That's the entire skill. It took four minutes to teach. **And it has been kept from
you for no reason other than that nobody's incentive was to teach it.**

## MODULE 4 — THE RATE TRAP (23:20–29:00) ⏱

**[033]** Two corrections, fast.

**The Federal Reserve does not set mortgage rates.** The Fed sets an overnight
bank-to-bank rate — that moves credit cards and car loans. Thirty-year mortgages
track the **bond market.** Proof you can check: there have been Fed cuts where
mortgage rates went *up* the same day.

And **seven things set your rate**, not one: credit score, down payment, program,
occupancy, property type, term, and points.

Which is why "what's your rate today" is unanswerable. Not evasive — unanswerable.
It's like asking a doctor "what's the dose." Anyone who fires back a number without
knowing five of those seven is quoting you a rate you may not qualify for. **That's
not a quote. That's bait.**

**[034]** But rate differences convert into something concrete, and this is the one to
memorize.

On a loan this size: **half a percent of rate is about a hundred forty dollars a
month — and a hundred forty a month is about twenty-three thousand dollars of
house.**

So when rates move half a point, the question isn't "should I panic." It's: do I
still want this house at twenty-three thousand more, or do I want a house that costs
twenty-three thousand less? That's a decision. "Rates went up" is weather.

**[035]** 💬 Two lenders, same day, same loan. Loan A: six seven five, no points.
Loan B: six two five — but two points, which is **eighty-five hundred fifty dollars**
at closing.

Payment: twenty-seven seventy-three versus twenty-six thirty-two.

**Which one's better? A or B in the chat.**

*(Wait. Most say B.)*

Almost everyone says B. And B might be right. But you can't tell yet, because nobody
has told you the only thing that decides it.

**[036]** 🎬 *(APR Calculator — run-book: `../resources/apr-demo.md`)*

Here they are with the APRs. Loan B still looks better — because APR assumes you keep
this loan thirty years.

Now watch what happens when I change one thing. **How long you actually keep it.**

*(Drag slowly: 30 → 7 → 5 → 3.)*

At three years, **Loan A wins.** At five, a tie. At seven, B pulls ahead. Same two
loans. **The winner changes based on a number that appears on neither Loan Estimate.**

**[037]** Eighty-five fifty in points. A hundred forty-one a month saved. Divide.

**Sixty-one months.** Five years and one month.

That's the whole analysis. Keep this loan longer and the points won. Shorter, and
you lit eighty-five hundred dollars on fire for a lower number on a piece of paper.

And it's the **loan**, not the house — refinancing ends the loan even if you never
move. If your honest answer is "I don't know how long," that's not a failure. That's
information, and it argues **against** paying points.

One more thing nobody offers: this trade runs backwards too. **A lender credit** —
you take a slightly higher rate and the lender pays some of your closing costs. For a
cash-tight first-time buyer that's frequently the right move. Ask for it.

**[038]** 🎬 *(Amortization — run-book: `../resources/amortization-demo.md`)*

Last one. Payment one of three hundred sixty. Twenty-seven oh two — of which
**twenty-three fifteen is interest** and three eighty-seven is principal. Eighty-three
percent of your first payment is rent on money.

And it doesn't flip fast. The lines cross at **month two eighteen.** Year eighteen.

Total interest over thirty years: about **five hundred forty-five thousand dollars.**

*(🔴 **PAUSE. TWO FULL SECONDS. DO NOT FILL IT.**)*

Okay. Now the lever — and it's why I don't mind showing you the scary number.

*(Type 100.)*

**One hundred dollars a month.** Paid off in about twenty-seven years instead of
thirty. Interest drops to about four eighty-one. That's **sixty-four thousand three
hundred dollars** back, for a hundred bucks a month.

And unlike everything else today — no lender, no approval, no market. **That one's
entirely yours.**

Two cautions on the way out. Confirm your servicer applies it to **principal** — some
apply it to next month's payment, which does nothing. And don't do this before you
have an emergency fund. Money in the house is hard to get back out.

## MODULE 5 — THE APPROVAL YOU CAN LOSE (29:00–32:10) ⏱

**[040]** Show of hands in the chat — who knows their credit score?

*(wait)*

Okay. Almost none of you know your **mortgage** credit score.

Different model — your banking app usually shows a consumer-education score;
mortgage lending uses mortgage-specific versions, and they can differ meaningfully in
either direction. Three bureaus, and they use **the middle one** — not the average.
And with two borrowers, generally **the lower** of the two middles drives pricing.

Maya's 742, Dev's 758. They get priced off **742.**

Which leads somewhere uncomfortable: sometimes a couple qualifies better with only
one borrower on the loan. Not always — you lose the second income. But it's a real
calculation. Ask for it both ways.

**[041]** 💬 True or false: closing a credit card you don't use helps your score.

*(Results come in heavily "true.")*

**False.** And it gets almost everybody. Closing an account shortens your average
history and removes available credit — which raises your utilization. The instinct to
clean up your credit before applying is the right instinct pointed at the wrong
thing.

Utilization is about **thirty percent** of your score and it moves in **thirty to
sixty days.** That's the lever. Pay balances under thirty percent per card, ideally
under ten. Per card, not just overall. Don't close anything. Fix real errors, but do
it **early** — an active dispute flag can stall underwriting.

And you don't need a 780. Pricing moves in tiers. 735 to 745 can matter. 780 to 800
usually does nothing.

**[042]** Nine days before closing, a client bought furniture. Nine thousand dollars,
zero percent for twenty-four months, because it was zero percent and he was excited.
And honestly — who wouldn't be. He was about to have a house with nothing in it.

Lenders re-pull credit before closing. **Every time.** His ratios moved, and his
approval — already issued — came apart.

*(beat — sympathy, no mockery)*

He wasn't reckless. **He bought a couch for a house he'd already been approved for.
Nobody told him the approval was still alive.**

So — eight things, from application to keys. No new credit, of any kind. Don't close
accounts. No large purchases. No job changes. Don't move money without a paper trail.
Don't accept a cash gift without telling your lender first. Don't co-sign. Don't miss
a payment.

**From application to keys, your financial life is a museum exhibit. Look, don't
touch.**

And the escape valve: if something *has* to change — a job offer, an inheritance, a
car that died — that's fine, life happens. **Call your loan officer first.** Almost
every one of these is survivable with warning and fatal as a surprise.

## MODULE 6 — THE BUILDER (32:10–34:30) ⏱

**[043]** If you're looking at new construction, two things.

**One — builders would rather give you twenty thousand in incentives than take
twenty thousand off the price**, because a price cut sets a comp that lowers every
other house in the subdivision. So don't negotiate price. **Negotiate everything
else, and negotiate hard.** Closing costs. Rate buydowns. Upgrades. Appliances.
Landscaping. Fencing. Lot premium.

**Two, and this one bites.** Your first-year property tax bill may be assessed on an
**unimproved lot.** Then the house gets assessed, your escrow payment jumps, and you
get a bill for the shortfall — both at once. **Ask in writing what the tax basis will
be at full assessment**, and budget from that number.

**[044]** He called me on a Tuesday and I could hear he'd already decided. He said,
**"I like you. But I'm not turning down fifteen thousand dollars to be loyal to a
guy I met on a webinar."**

Which — fair.

His builder was offering fifteen thousand toward closing costs if he used their
lender. So we ran it. Their rate was a quarter point higher — about seventy dollars a
month, and over the seven years he expected to stay, about fifty-nine hundred
dollars. Plus roughly three thousand more in Section A.

So the fifteen-thousand-dollar credit was really worth about **six thousand one
hundred.**

**And it still won. He used their lender. And I told him to.**

*(beat)*

I'm telling you that on purpose, because I want you to hear it from somebody with the
opposite incentive. **"Don't use the builder's lender" is literally my business
interest.** So hear it from me: sometimes the builder's lender is the right answer.

What's never right is taking the incentive without running the comparison — and the
reason builders can offer these at all is that hardly anybody does. Get their Loan
Estimate. Get one outside estimate. Compare Section A. Convert the rate difference to
dollars over how long you'll keep the loan. **Ten minutes.**

Twelve questions in your download kit. Three to remember: **what's the incentive if I
*don't* use your lender. Can I take a price reduction instead. And can I get an
independent inspection** — before drywall and again before closing. People skip that
because the house is new. New houses are built by humans on deadlines.

## THE LANDING (34:30–38:00)

**[045]** Nine mistakes. You've met all of them today. Fast.

**One** — looking at houses before talking to a lender. I've watched people lose a
house in six hours over this.
**Two** — shopping the payment instead of the total cost.
**Three** — only talking to one lender. Six thousand four hundred dollars.
**Four** — new credit between application and closing. The sofa.
**Five** — confusing closing costs with cash to close.
**Six** — waiting years for twenty percent. The Post-it.
**Seven** — buying at the very top of your approval. A hundred and five thousand

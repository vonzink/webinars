# Stories

Eight stories, one per module. Every one carries a **number** — the number is what
survives in memory after the narrative fades.

**Disclosure rule:** these are composites drawn from real patterns, with details
changed. Never use an identifiable client, and never imply a specific outcome is
typical or promised. Introduce as "a client" or by an invented first name — never
a real one.

**Craft rule:** ≤45 seconds. One specific sensory detail. One number. One turn.
Do not explain the moral — the next slide is the moral.

---

## Story 1 — The Reyes Offer
**Module 2 · Slide 009 · Number: 6 hours**

Marcus and Dani looked for five months. Found the one on a Saturday, called their
agent from the driveway. Agent asked for the pre-approval letter. They didn't have
one — it was on the list. Took six days to get. The house went under contract in
**six hours** to someone who'd done the boring part first.

**Turn:** They had *more* money than the winning buyer. They lost on sequence, not
strength.
**Teaches:** Pre-approval before shopping. Mistake #1.
**Detail to keep:** calling from the driveway. It's the moment everyone recognizes.

## Story 2 — $520,000 / $390,000
**Module 3 · Slide 018 · Number: $900/month**

Priya was pre-approved for $520,000 and bought at $390,000. Two years later: a
transmission, a layoff round she survived, and a sister who needed help — same
spring. She handled all three.

**Her line (the whole story):** *"I didn't buy a smaller house. I bought a smaller
amount of fear."*
**Turn:** The industry approves you at the top because guidelines measure
*repayment*, not *okayness*.
**Teaches:** Approved ≠ comfortable. Mistake #7.
**Warning:** Do not moralize. She wasn't scared — she ran the math. If it plays as
"be timid," it's being delivered wrong.

## Story 3 — Tom & Ashley's Six Years
**Module 4 · Slide 033 · Number: 6 years**

Saved six years to hit 20%. Real discipline, and they made it. But prices rose
while they saved, so the 20% target rose with them — it's a percentage of a moving
number. Six more years of rent, zero equity. They were eligible for 3% down the
entire time.

**Turn:** They spent six years avoiding a *temporary* fee.
**Required counterweight — do not cut:** *"If they'd bought right before a
downturn this story reads differently. Waiting isn't always wrong. But it should
be a decision, not a default."* Without this the story is propaganda.
**Teaches:** The 20% myth. Mistake #6.

## Story 4 — "It Was Only Four Years"
**Module 4 · Slide 036 · Number: 34 years old, never asked**

First call, scraping together 3.5% for FHA. Mentions offhand he did four years in
the Army after high school. Stop the call: you can buy with nothing down and no
monthly mortgage insurance. He was 34. Nobody had ever asked him.

**Turn:** He almost paid tens of thousands of dollars for the privilege of not
asking a question.
**Teaches:** VA eligibility, and the meta-lesson — ask anyway.
**Delivery:** This is the most emotionally efficient story in the deck. Don't
oversell it. The flatter you tell it, the harder it hits.

## Story 5 — Same Rate, $6,400 Apart
**Module 6 · Slide 053 · Number: $6,400**

Two Loan Estimates. Same term, same amount, **same rate**. She'd written it off as
a coin flip. Section A: ~$1,300 vs. ~$7,700.

**Turn:** *"She wasn't defrauded. Every number was disclosed correctly on the
government form. The disclosure worked perfectly. The disclosure only works if
somebody reads page two."*
**Teaches:** Section A. Shop lenders. Mistake #3.
**Delivery:** The turn is the payload. Slow down on "the disclosure worked
perfectly."

## Story 6 — $5,400 and $31,760
**Module 6 · Slide 059 · Number: $4,100 short**

He knew his closing costs were $5,400. He'd read page one. He showed up at the
title company **$4,100 short** — in the parking lot, calling his brother.

**Turn:** Closing costs and cash to close are not the same word, and on this loan
they're $26,000 apart.
**Teaches:** Cash to close. Mistake #5.
**Delivery:** "In the parking lot, calling his brother" is the line. Keep it.

---

## Story 7 — The $9,000 Sofa
**Module 7 · Slide 064 · Number: 9 days**

Nine days before closing, financed $9,000 of furniture at 0% because it was 0% and
he was excited. Lenders re-pull credit before closing. Ratios moved. Approval came
apart. Didn't close on time.

**Turn:** He wasn't reckless. He bought a couch for a house he was already approved
for. Nobody told him the approval was still alive.
**Teaches:** The do-not-do list. Mistake #4.
**Delivery:** Genuine sympathy, zero mockery. Half the room was about to do this.

## Story 8 — The Builder's $15,000
**Module 8 · Slide 067 · Number: $15,000 vs. ~$6,100 — and it still won**

Builder offers $15,000 in closing costs to use their lender. Rate a quarter point
higher (~$70/mo, ~$5,900 over 7 years) plus ~$3,000 more in Section A. Net value
of the incentive: about **$6,100**. He took it. **I told him to.**

**Turn:** The story where I lose the deal and say so on camera.
**Teaches:** Run the comparison. Mistake #8. And — the speaker's own bias, named.
**Required:** Say the conflict of interest out loud. *"'Don't use the builder's
lender' is literally my business interest, so hear this from me specifically."*
Cutting that turns the module into the thing this webinar exists to replace.

## Bench (not in the 40-minute cut)

- **The dispute flag** — cleaning up credit *during* underwriting stalled a closing
  for three weeks. Use if credit Q&A goes long.
- **The gift letter** — $10,000 from a parent, deposited without documentation,
  became a two-week paper chase. Use for asset-sourcing questions.
- **The self-employed year** — great year, wrote it all off, qualified on nothing.
  Use if a self-employed buyer asks in Q&A. This one is common and lands hard.
- **The appraisal gap** — came in $12,000 low; who pays. Use for competitive-market
  questions.

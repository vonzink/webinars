## MODULE 2 — THE PROCESS (2:30–7:00) ⏱

**[009]** Marcus and Dani spent five months looking. Every weekend. They knew the
school boundaries, they knew which streets got afternoon sun, they had opinions
about countertops that I did not understand.

They walked into a house on a Saturday and both of them just — stopped. It was the
one. They called their agent from the driveway. Agent says, great, let's write it up
— send me your pre-approval letter.

*(beat)*

They didn't have one. They'd been meaning to. It was on the list.

It took them six days to get one. The house went under contract in — **six hours.**
To somebody who'd done the boring part first.

*(beat)*

Here's what I want you to take from that. They didn't lose that house because they
had less money. They had **more** money than the buyer who got it. They lost it
because they did step four before step one.

**[010]** Nine stages. This is the whole thing.

Financial readiness. Pre-approval. House hunting. Offer. Application. Processing.
Underwriting. Clear to close. Closing.

*(beat)*

Notice where "look at houses" is. **Third.** Not first.

The whole industry lets you believe it's first, because looking at houses is fun and
getting pre-approved feels like a test. That's **mistake number one** — hold onto
that, we're coming back to it at the end.

**[011]** The first call with a lender is not an application. Nothing is committed.
Nobody is judging you. It's twenty minutes of: here's what I make, here's what I
owe, here's what I've saved — what does that make possible?

And I'll tell you the thing that actually keeps people from making that call.
They're afraid of hearing no.

*(beat)*

So let me take that away. **"No" is almost never the answer. "Not yet, and here's
exactly what changes it" is the answer.** In my experience the average "not yet" is
between three and nine months of specific, boring, do-able steps. Pay this down.
Wait out that. Document this.

The only way that becomes three *years* is if you never made the call.

**[012]** Three different documents, and the industry uses the words loosely —
sometimes on purpose.

Prequalification is a conversation. Worth almost nothing to a seller. Pre-approval —
credit pulled, documents collected, usually automated underwriting. This is what
most sellers expect. Underwritten approval — a human underwriter has already
reviewed you. The only thing left to approve is the house, not you.

In a competitive situation that third one is a weapon. So if you're going to be
competing, ask your lender for it by name: **"Can you get me underwritten up
front?"** Not everybody offers it. Now you know to ask.

**[013]** Seven people in your transaction, and they do not all work for you.

The listing agent is going to be lovely to you. They are also legally not yours. The
appraiser is deliberately walled off so neither side can lean on them — that's a
feature. The underwriter decides everything and you will never speak to them.

*(beat)*

And here's maybe the most useful sentence in this whole webinar: **"Who pays you,
and how?"** Ask everybody. Nobody good is offended by that question. That's
**mistake number nine.**

**[014]** Thirty to forty-five days is typical from contract to keys.

The part nobody warns you about is the middle. Days ten through twenty-five, you're
going to get emails asking for things you already sent. It feels like they think
you're lying to them.

They don't. Underwriting is a checklist, and the checklist doesn't know what you
already did. Send it again, same day, and it's a non-event. Send it in four days and
it delays your closing. That's genuinely the whole difference.

Two dates on here are federal protections, not courtesies. Loan Estimate within
three business days of applying. Closing Disclosure at least three business days
before you sign. That second one exists so you have time to actually read it.

**[015]** Five things that kill deals. Fast.

**New debt** — the car, the furniture, the credit card. That's **mistake number
four**, and it has its own segment at minute thirty-one.

**Job change** — especially W-2 to self-employed. Congratulations, and please close
first.

**Large unexplained deposits.** Every dollar has to have a story. If your mom is
helping you, that's completely fine — tell your lender *before* the money lands.

**Low appraisal** — somebody has to cover the gap.

**Missed deadlines.** Your inspection date and your financing date are contractual
dates, not suggestions.

**[016]** That map is a one-page PDF in your download kit. Print it. Put it on the
fridge. Because when you're on day fourteen and you feel like nothing is happening —
it's because you're in underwriting, which looks exactly like nothing happening.

Okay. You know the road now. Which brings us to the thing that actually keeps people
up at night — and it isn't the process. It's the number.

## MODULE 3 — WHAT CAN YOU AFFORD (7:00–13:00) ⏱

**[018]** Priya came to me pre-approved for five hundred twenty thousand. Real
number, real letter, she qualified for every dollar of it.

She bought at three ninety.

And I want to be careful here, because I'm not telling you she was scared or that
she settled. She ran the math and decided she'd rather have nine hundred dollars a
month than a fourth bedroom.

Two years later her car needed a transmission, her company did a layoff round she
survived but did not enjoy, and her sister needed help. Same spring. All three. She
handled all of it and never missed a beat.

*(beat)*

She told me something I've repeated ever since. She said: **"I didn't buy a smaller
house. I bought a smaller amount of fear."**

*(beat)*

Here's the uncomfortable part, and I'm including myself in this. **The mortgage
industry approves you at the top of your range.** That's not a conspiracy — it's that
our guidelines measure whether you'll *repay*, not whether you'll be *okay*. Those
are different questions, and only one of them is being asked on your behalf.

That's **mistake number seven.** So you're going to ask the other one. Right now.

**[019]** Everybody, right now. Piece of paper, phone notes, back of an envelope.

Write down the highest monthly housing payment you could pay **every single month
for the next ten years** — and still take the trip, still fix the car, still say yes
when somebody asks you to dinner.

Don't compute anything. Gut number. Write it down and hold onto it.

*(🔴 **15 SECONDS OF SILENCE. Say nothing. It will feel long. That's the exercise.**)*

**[020]** There are two budgets in this transaction and people conflate them
constantly.

The bank's budget asks: will you repay? It looks at gross income and documented
debt, and it ignores childcare, groceries, retirement, travel, pets, and the fact
that your dog is nine.

Your budget asks: will you be **okay**? It ignores nothing.

*(beat)*

One of these is printed on your pre-approval letter. The other one is your actual
life. **Nobody in the transaction is going to compute the second one for you.**
Today you're going to.

**[021]** P-I-T-I. Four parts. Principal — the part that actually buys the house.
Interest — rent on the money. Taxes. Insurance.

That last arrangement, where your lender collects your taxes and insurance monthly
and pays those bills for you, is called **escrow.** And it's the most misunderstood
thing in this business. Escrow is a subscription your lender runs on your behalf.
**It is not a fee. It's not their money.**

But here's the part that surprises people: **your payment will change.** When the tax
bill changes — usually up, usually once a year — your escrow adjusts. "Fixed rate"
does not mean "fixed payment." Now it won't be a surprise.

**[022]** Let's stop being abstract. For the rest of this webinar we are buying
**one house.** Same house, all forty minutes.

Four hundred fifty thousand dollars. Five percent down — twenty-two thousand five
hundred. Loan amount four hundred twenty-seven thousand five hundred. Thirty-year
fixed at six and a half percent, which is a made-up number for teaching, not a
quote.

Principal and interest: **twenty-seven hundred and two dollars.** Taxes, two-oh-six.
Insurance, one-fifty. Mortgage insurance, one twenty-five.

**Thirty-one eighty-three.** Say that out loud once, because we're going to see it
about eleven more times.

And if this house had an HOA — a lot of Colorado condos and newer neighborhoods do —
add two hundred fifty. HOA dues count against you exactly like a mortgage payment,
and they can be raised without your vote.

**[023]** Five things no calculator includes.

**Maintenance.** One to two percent of value per year. And it will not be smooth —
nothing for two years and then a fourteen-thousand-dollar roof.

**Utilities.** A house is not an apartment.

**Furnishing** — the empty-house tax. Three to ten thousand dollars, and it is the
number one reason people put furniture on a credit card in month one. Remember that.
It comes back at minute thirty-one.

**Commute.** Cheaper house, farther out.

**Escalation.** Taxes and insurance go up. Insurance in Colorado has gone up
meaningfully. Assume increases.

*(beat)*

So Maya and Dev's real number isn't thirty-one eighty-three. It's about
**thirty-six thirty-three.** They're renting at twenty-one fifty. That's not a
thousand-dollar change. **It's fourteen eighty-three.**

And one more: **the emergency fund is not part of the down payment.** If buying the
house requires spending it, you're buying too much house.

**[024]** Debt-to-income. It is the single number that decides how much house you
get, and almost nobody outside this business can define it.

Everything you're contractually obligated to pay every month, divided by your gross
monthly income — before taxes.

Think of it like a **weight limit on a suitcase.** The airline doesn't care what's in
the bag. It cares what it weighs. Underwriting doesn't care whether that five
hundred dollars is a car you love or a car you regret.

**[025]** Maya and Dev make ninety-five hundred a month gross, with seven eighty in
other debt.

Front-end — housing alone — **thirty-three and a half percent.** Back-end — housing
plus everything — **forty-one point seven.**

Back-end is the one that matters. Conventional commonly goes to about forty-five,
and with strong automated-underwriting findings can stretch toward fifty. FHA can go
higher with compensating factors. VA doesn't use a hard cap the same way — it uses
**residual income**, which is honestly a smarter test.

*(beat)*

But watch this. At forty-one seven, they're comfortably approvable. **They could go
to forty-five and buy about sixty thousand dollars more house.** Their DTI would
still say yes.

Their life would say something else. **The ratio has no opinion about their life.**

**[026]** Fast, because people always ask.

Income that counts: salary, hourly at your average hours, overtime and bonus and
commission on a two-year average, self-employment, documented rental. Doesn't count:
cash tips you don't report, a promised raise, a side gig with no history, and money
your parents "will help with."

Debt that counts: car, student loans — even deferred — card minimums, personal
loans, support you pay, and co-signed loans **you don't even pay.**

And here's the one that hurts. **Childcare doesn't count.** Daycare can be fifteen
hundred a month and underwriting will not see a dollar of it. **Your budget has to.**

One more, for the self-employed: **write-offs.** If you had a great year on paper and
wrote it all down to nothing for taxes, we qualify you on the nothing. Talk to a
lender the year *before* you buy.

**[027]** *(DEMO 1 — Budget Calculator. Run-book: `resources/budget-demo.md`.)*

**[028]** *(POLL 2.)* Now compare that to the number you wrote down at minute eight.
For most people the written one is lower. That's your gut, and your gut did that
**before** it knew what houses cost. Your gut is usually the honest one.

**[029]** Artifact number two, and this one is free.

For three months before you buy: every month, transfer the difference between your
rent and your projected all-in payment into a separate savings account. For Maya and
Dev that's fourteen eighty-three.

Two things happen. One — you find out whether it's actually livable, for free,
instead of finding out in month four of a thirty-year loan. Two — at the end you
have **forty-four hundred and forty-nine dollars** toward your closing costs.

**This test pays you to take it.**

*(AMA BREAK 1 — 45 seconds.)*

So now you know your number. Which raises the obvious next problem — and it's the
one that stops more first-time buyers than anything else. Not the payment. The pile
of cash you think you need before anyone will even talk to you.

## MODULE 4 — DOWN PAYMENT & PROGRAMS (13:00–18:00) ⏱

**[031]** *(POLL 3 — hold the results. Do not comment.)*

**[032]** So — a chunk of this room just told me they need ninety thousand dollars.

*(beat)*

The real number, for most of you, is somewhere between **zero and fifteen thousand
seven hundred fifty.**

VA — zero down. USDA — zero down. Conventional — three percent, thirteen five. FHA —
three and a half, fifteen seven fifty.

*(beat)*

Now — where did twenty percent come from? It's not made up. **At twenty percent down
you avoid mortgage insurance.** That's a real benefit, and that's the origin.

Somewhere along the way, "avoids one specific fee" got translated into "required."
And that translation has cost a generation of buyers more money than it ever saved
them. Let me show you exactly how.

**[033]** Tom and Ashley decided they'd do it right. Twenty percent, no shortcuts.
Heads down, and they saved.

It took six years. And they did it. I admire the discipline, because it is real
discipline.

*(beat)*

But here's what happened during those six years. Prices went up. So the twenty
percent target went up with them — it's a percentage of a moving number. **Every year
they saved, the finish line moved.** And they paid six more years of rent, which
bought them exactly zero equity.

Meanwhile, they were eligible for a three-percent-down conventional loan the entire
time. They'd have paid mortgage insurance — call it a bit over a hundred a month —
for a handful of years until they hit twenty percent equity. Then it comes off.
Permanently.

**They spent six years avoiding a temporary fee.**

*(beat — tone shift)*

Now — the honest counterweight, because I told you I wouldn't sell you anything.
**This is not a law of nature.** If they'd bought right before a downturn, this story
reads differently. Waiting is not always wrong.

**But it should be a decision, not a default.** Theirs was a default. They never ran
the comparison, because nobody ever told them there was one to run. That's **mistake
number six.**

**[034]** Conventional 97. Three percent down, thirty-year fixed, generally requires
at least one first-time buyer.

And "first-time buyer" does not mean what you think. Most programs define it as **no
ownership interest in a primary residence in the last three years.** So if you owned
something a decade ago, you're probably a first-time buyer again.

HomeReady and Home Possible. Three percent down, income limits by area — and here's
what matters: **reduced mortgage insurance coverage and better pricing** at the same
loan-to-value. If you're income-eligible, these are frequently the best product
available to a first-time buyer, and they are **chronically under-mentioned.** Ask
about them by name.

Homebuyer education is generally required — it's an online course. Do it early.

And the big one: **conventional mortgage insurance is removable.**

**[035]** FHA. Three and a half percent down at 580. More forgiving on credit
history, collections, past derogatory events. Higher DTI tolerance in a lot of
scenarios. It is a genuinely great program. I'm not talking you out of it.

*(beat)*

But here's the tradeoff nobody mentions. There's an upfront premium financed into
the loan, and an annual one paid monthly. And **on most FHA loans with less than ten
percent down, that annual premium lasts the life of the loan.** Not until twenty
percent equity. The life of the loan.

So the honest framing: FHA's monthly cost is often competitive, sometimes better —
especially if your credit is in the 600s. **But conventional's mortgage insurance has
an exit and FHA's usually doesn't.**

Which means for a lot of FHA buyers the plan isn't "FHA forever." It's **FHA to get
in, refinance out when it makes sense.** Completely legitimate strategy. Just choose
it **on purpose**, knowing the exit depends on future rates. **Nobody can promise you
that refinance. Anyone who does is selling.**

**[036]** A few years back I'm on a first call with a guy. Good job, decent credit,
scraping together three and a half percent for an FHA loan. Halfway through, totally
offhand, he mentions he'd "done four years in the Army after high school."

I stopped the call. I said — you know you can buy this house with nothing down? And
no monthly mortgage insurance at all?

*(beat)*

He didn't. **He was thirty-four. Nobody had ever asked him.**

*(beat)*

VA loans. Zero down, no monthly mortgage insurance — the two biggest costs, gone.
And it qualifies on **residual income** — how much cash is actually left after
everything, given your family size and where you live.

There's a funding fee, it's financeable, and it's **waived** for veterans receiving
compensation for a service-connected disability. And it's **reusable** — not once per
lifetime.

Active duty, veterans, **National Guard, Reserves**, certain surviving spouses. Get
your Certificate of Eligibility.

And if you're thinking "it was only a few years, it probably doesn't count" — **ask
anyway.** That's the whole lesson. He almost paid tens of thousands of dollars for
the privilege of not asking a question.

**[037]** USDA. Zero down, in eligible areas, under a household income limit.

Two things people get wrong. **"Rural" does not mean a farm** — a lot of Colorado
towns that feel like completely normal towns are eligible. People self-disqualify on
vibes. And **the boundary is a map** that gets updated. Check the address, not the
assumption.

**[038]** This is the part you cannot find on a national website.

**Layer one — CHFA.** The Colorado Housing and Finance Authority. First mortgage plus
down payment assistance, as a grant or a second mortgage, layered on top of
conventional, FHA, VA, or USDA. Income and price limits by county. Homebuyer
education required. Participating lenders only.

**Layer two — metro and city.** Several Front Range municipalities run their own,
sometimes with better terms.

**Layer three — county.** Varies enormously. Worth ten minutes.

**Layer four — and nobody checks this one — employer and profession.** Teachers,
first responders, healthcare workers. Large employers. Unions, credit unions.
**Nobody ever asks their HR department whether their employer has a housing benefit.
Some of you have one. It costs you one email.**

*(beat — obligation)*

Here's my obligation to you. **Program terms, income limits, and available funding
change, and some of these run out of money mid-year.** Anything specific I told you
today could be stale by the time you act on it.

So the artifact isn't a list of programs. It's the instruction: **ask your lender to
run the DPA layers for your county and your income, on the day you're actually
shopping.** A broker can check across all of them. Ask by name.

**[039]** Four kinds of mortgage insurance, and they're not interchangeable. The one
that matters: **BPMI on conventional is removable.** Three routes.

**Request removal at eighty percent** — you have to ask, nobody calls you.
**Automatic termination at seventy-eight percent**, by the original schedule.
**Appreciation-based**, with a new appraisal — and here I have to hedge: **servicer
and investor rules vary.** Not an entitlement. Get the policy in writing.

On Maya and Dev's loan, eighty percent of four-fifty is three hundred sixty
thousand. They need sixty-seven thousand five hundred of paydown — or appreciation.
**That hundred twenty-five a month is not forever. It's a phase.**

Five questions. Did you or your spouse serve? VA, check it first. USDA-eligible area
and under the income limit? USDA. Credit under about six-sixty or recent derogatory
history? FHA is likely. Under the HomeReady income limit? Probably your best one.
None of the above? Conventional, and compare against FHA on total cost.

**Then layer down payment assistance on top.** DPA isn't a program you pick instead.
It's a floor you stack under the one you picked.

So the down payment is smaller than you thought. Now let's talk about the number
everybody obsesses over and almost nobody understands.

## MODULE 5 — RATES, APR & BUYDOWNS (18:00–24:30) ⏱

**[041]** Let's kill the biggest one first. **The Federal Reserve does not set
mortgage rates.**

The Fed sets an overnight bank-to-bank rate. It influences credit cards, HELOCs, car
loans. Thirty-year mortgage rates track the **bond market** — mortgage-backed
securities, roughly with the ten-year Treasury.

And here's the proof you can check yourself: there have been Fed cuts where mortgage
rates went **up** the same day. If the Fed set them, that couldn't happen.

What actually moves them: investor demand for mortgage bonds. Inflation expectations
— inflation eats a fixed return, so investors demand more. Employment data. And the
spread between mortgage bonds and Treasuries.

*(beat)*

And the honest conclusion: **nobody knows where rates are going.** Not me, not your
agent, not the guy on the podcast. Every forecast for the last several years has
been revised. Any strategy that requires you to be right about the direction of
rates is not a strategy. **It's a wager.**

**[042]** The market sets a base. Your file adjusts it. Seven things.

Credit score — the biggest lever, and it moves in **tiers**, not smoothly.
Loan-to-value. Program. Occupancy. Property type. Term. Points.

*(beat)*

Which is why "what's your rate today" is an unanswerable question. Not evasive —
**unanswerable.** It's like asking a doctor "what's the dose." Dose of what, for
whom? Anyone who fires back a number without knowing five of those seven things is
quoting you a rate you may not qualify for. **That's not a quote. That's bait.**

**[043]** Maya and Dev's loan. Five and a half percent, twenty-four twenty-seven.
Six percent, twenty-five sixty-three. Six and a half, twenty-seven oh two. Seven,
twenty-eight forty-four. Seven and a half, twenty-nine ninety.

Memorize this **shape**, because it's portable. **Half a percent of rate is roughly a
hundred forty dollars a month — and a hundred forty a month is roughly twenty-three
thousand dollars of purchase price.**

That's the useful conversion. When rates move half a point, the question isn't
"should I panic." It's: **do I still want this house at twenty-three thousand more —
or do I want a house that costs twenty-three thousand less?** That's a decision you
can make. "Rates went up" is just weather.

**[044]** Two numbers on every mortgage disclosure. They are never the same, and
almost nobody can tell you why.

Interest rate — what your payment is calculated from. APR — the rate plus certain
lender costs, spread across the full term.

The **rate is the sticker price on the car. The APR is closer to the out-the-door
price.** Two dealers can advertise the same sticker and hand you very different final
numbers, because one has six thousand dollars of fees stapled to the back page.

APR exists because lenders figured out you'd shop on rate alone. It's imperfect. It's
still the best single comparison number you have.

**[045]** Same house. Same loan amount. Two lenders, same day.

Loan A — six point seven five, no points. Loan B — six point two five, but two
points, which is **eighty-five hundred and fifty dollars** at closing.

Payment: twenty-seven seventy-three versus **twenty-six thirty-two.**

*(beat)*

Which one's better?

*(Wait for chat. Most say B.)*

Almost everyone says B. And B **might** be better. But you cannot tell yet, because
nobody has told you the only thing that decides it.

**[046]** APR is flawed in three specific ways. **One — it assumes you keep the loan
thirty years.** Most people don't come close. **Two — ARMs**, where it has to guess at
future adjustments. **Three — lenders don't all include identical items.**

So: **use APR as a flag, not a verdict.** A big gap between rate and APR means big
upfront costs. That's what it's for.

**[047]** *(DEMO 2 — APR Calculator. Run-book: `resources/apr-demo.md`.)*

**[048]** One point is one percent of the loan amount, paid at closing, to
permanently lower your rate. On four twenty-seven five, one point is **four thousand
two hundred seventy-five dollars.**

Like buying a **gym membership for the year instead of monthly** — cheaper per month,
a total waste if you move in March.

Now the part almost nobody tells first-time buyers: **the same trade runs backwards.**
A **lender credit.** You take a higher rate, and the lender pays some of your closing
costs. That is frequently the right move for a buyer who is cash-tight — which is
most first-time buyers. **Cash today can be worth more than a slightly lower
payment. Nobody offers this. Ask for it.**

**[049]** Eighty-five hundred and fifty in points. A hundred forty-one dollars a
month saved. Divide.

**Sixty-one months.** Five years and one month.

*(beat)*

That's the whole analysis. Keep this exact loan longer, the points won. Shorter, and
they lit eighty-five hundred dollars on fire for a lower number on a piece of paper.

So the real question is: **how long will you keep this specific loan?** Not this
house. **This loan.** Because refinancing ends the loan even if you never move.

First-time buyers systematically overestimate this. And if your honest answer is "I
don't know" — that's not a failure. That's information, and it argues **against**
paying points.

**Compute the breakeven in months. If you won't clearly pass it, don't.**

**[050]** A **2-1 buydown**: year one your rate acts about two percent lower, year
two about one percent lower, year three onward it's the real rate. Prefunded into
escrow, **usually paid by the seller or builder.**

The good: real cash-flow relief in the two most expensive years, often on somebody
else's money.

The trap: **you qualify at the real rate.** You will be paying that full payment in
year three. If the plan depends on refinancing before then, that plan depends on
rates you cannot control.

And when a **builder** offers this — sometimes a buydown is funded by a price that
was set high enough to pay for it. **A price reduction is permanent. A temporary
buydown is two years.** Ask which one you can have.

## MODULE 6 — THE LOAN ESTIMATE (24:30–30:00) ⏱

**[053]** A client sent me two Loan Estimates and asked which was better. Both
thirty-year fixed. Both the same loan amount. **Both exactly the same interest
rate.**

She'd already decided it was a coin flip.

*(beat)*

Section A on the first one — origination charges, the lender's own fees — was about
**thirteen hundred dollars.** Section A on the second was about **seven thousand
seven hundred.**

Same rate. **Six thousand four hundred dollars apart.** On page two, in a box neither
of them had drawn her attention to.

*(slow down)*

She wasn't being defrauded. Every number was disclosed correctly, on the government
form, exactly as required. **The disclosure worked perfectly.**

**The disclosure only works if somebody reads page two.**

*(beat)*

That's **mistake number three.** And in the next four minutes you're going to learn
to read page two. It is the highest-paid four minutes of this webinar.

**[054]** The Loan Estimate. Three pages, federally standardized since 2015. **Every
lender in America has to give you the same form, in the same order.**

That's the gift. Before this existed, comparison was basically impossible. Now it's
trivial — *if you know where to look.*

Two things you're owed. You must receive it within three business days of a
completed application. And certain costs are subject to tolerance limits — they can't
just go up, and if they do, the lender generally has to cure the difference.

And the sentence that matters most: **asking for a Loan Estimate does not obligate
you to anything.** Get three. Genuinely, get three.

**[055]** Page one. Five boxes.

Loan Terms — and a set of yes/no columns. On a standard thirty-year fixed those
should all be the boring answer. **If any of them surprise you, stop and ask why.**

Projected Payments. Taxes and insurance. **Costs at Closing — and there are two
different numbers in this box.** Rate lock.

One rule: **if you compare two Loan Estimates issued on different days, you're not
comparing lenders. You're comparing days.** Same day. Ask for it.

**[056]** Page two. Only two boxes matter.

**Section A — Origination Charges.** The lender's own fees plus points. **This is the
box.** This is where it was thirteen hundred at one lender and seven thousand seven
hundred at another.

**Section B** — appraisal, credit report, flood cert. Everybody pays roughly the
same.

**Section C — services you CAN shop for.** Title, settlement, survey. And here's the
leverage nobody uses: **the lender has to hand you a Written List of Providers, and
you're allowed to bring your own.** Four-figure variance in the same city. **Almost
nobody shops it.**

E through G are mostly not the lender's doing. Nobody's competing on your property
taxes.

So: **Section A is the labor line on a repair estimate.** Parts cost what parts cost.
Labor is where shops differ.

**[057]** Two Loan Estimates. Same loan amount. Same rate. Same day. Forty-five
seconds — find the money. Section letter in the chat.

*(🔴 Let the timer run. Say nothing for 30 seconds.)*

**Section A.** Every time. That's the muscle. When somebody hands you a Loan
Estimate, your eyes go to **page two, Section A**, before anywhere else.

**[058]** Closing costs and prepaids get lumped together and they're completely
different animals.

**Closing costs** — money spent to get the loan. Gone. You bought a service.

**Prepaids and escrows** — that money is **still yours**, paid early. Prepaid
interest. Your first year of insurance. A few months of taxes as a cushion. **You're
not losing it — you're front-loading it.**

And a real tactic almost nobody tells first-time buyers: interest on this loan is
about **seventy-six dollars a day.** Close on the twenty-eighth instead of the third
and you've moved roughly **nineteen hundred dollars** of cash-to-close. Doesn't
reduce what you owe — but if cash at the table is your constraint, that's real.

**[059]** A buyer told me, very confidently, that his closing costs were fifty-four
hundred dollars. He was right. He'd read page one.

*(beat)*

He showed up at the title company with a cashier's check and was **four thousand one
hundred dollars short.** In the parking lot. Calling his brother.

Down payment twenty-two five. Closing costs fifty-four hundred. Prepaid interest
eleven forty-two. A year of insurance, eighteen hundred. Escrow reserves, nine
eighteen.

**Cash to close: thirty-one thousand seven hundred sixty dollars.**

*(beat)*

Closing costs: fifty-four hundred. Cash to close: thirty-one seven sixty. **Those are
not the same word, and they are twenty-six thousand dollars apart.**

That's **mistake number five.** The number you plan your life around is **Cash to
Close. Page one. Bottom right.** If you remember one physical location on one piece
of paper from today, make it that one.

**[060]** Six steps. Sixty seconds. Any two lenders.

Same day. Same loan amount, program, and lock period. **Compare Section A only.**
Note the points. Check Cash to Close. Ignore B, E, F, G.

*(beat, slow)*

And here's the part that should tell you something about this industry: **that's the
entire skill.** It took four minutes to teach. It has been kept from you for no
reason other than that nobody's incentive was to teach it.

*(AMA BREAK 2 — 45 seconds.)*

Which is the perfect handoff. Because the thing that determines what's in Section A
in the first place is a three-digit number you probably think you already know.

## MODULE 7 — CREDIT (30:00–33:30) ⏱

**[062]** Show of hands in the chat — who knows their credit score?

*(wait)*

Okay. **Almost none of you know your mortgage credit score.**

**One — different model.** The score in your banking app is usually a
consumer-education model. Mortgage lending uses mortgage-specific FICO versions.
They can differ meaningfully — sometimes twenty points or more, in either direction.

**Two — three bureaus, and they use the middle one.** Not the average. The middle.

**Three — two borrowers?** Generally the **lower** of the two middles drives pricing.

So Maya's a 742, Dev's a 758 — they get priced off **742.** Which leads somewhere
uncomfortable: **sometimes a couple qualifies for a better loan with only one
borrower on it.** Not always — you lose the second income. But it's a real
calculation. **Ask for it both ways.**

And the scoring model is changing. **Ask your lender what they're pulling today**,
because the answer has been moving.

**[063]** Five factors. Payment history, about thirty-five percent — slow.
**Utilization, about thirty percent — and it moves in thirty to sixty days.** Length
of history, new credit, credit mix.

**Utilization is the lever.**

*(POLL 4)* True or false — closing a credit card you don't use helps your score.

**False.** And this one gets almost everybody. Closing shortens your average history
and removes available credit, which raises utilization. The instinct to "clean up my
credit" is the right instinct pointed at the wrong thing.

So: pay balances down — under thirty percent per card, ideally under ten. **Per card,
not just overall.** Don't close anything. And dispute real errors, but **early** — an
active dispute flag can stall underwriting.

And the thing nobody says: **you do not need a 780.** Pricing moves in tiers. 735 to
745 can matter. 780 to 800 usually does nothing. Find out which tier you're in.

**[064]** The fear that stops people from shopping: won't three lenders wreck my
credit?

**No.** Mortgage inquiries within a shopping window — commonly fourteen to forty-five
days depending on the model — are treated as **one inquiry.** The system was
deliberately designed so you could shop.

Let me be blunt about who benefits from you not knowing that. **Getting three Loan
Estimates costs you essentially nothing in score.** Not getting them cost that woman
six thousand four hundred dollars.

*(EXERCISE 3)* How many credit applications have you opened in the last twelve
months? Card, car, store financing, phone plan, buy-now-pay-later. Number in the
chat.

*(then)*

Nine days before closing, a client bought furniture. Nine thousand dollars, zero
percent for twenty-four months, because it was zero percent and he was excited. And
honestly — who wouldn't be? He was about to have a house with nothing in it.

Lenders re-pull credit before closing. **Every time.** His ratios moved, and his
approval — already issued — came apart. He did not close on time.

*(beat — sympathy, no mockery)*

He didn't do anything reckless. **He bought a couch for a house he'd already been
approved for. Nobody told him the approval was still alive.**

Eight things, from application to keys. No new credit. Don't close accounts. No large
purchases. No job changes. No moving money without a paper trail. No cash gifts
without telling your lender first. Don't co-sign. Don't miss a payment.

**From application to keys, your financial life is a museum exhibit. Look, don't
touch.**

And the escape valve: if something **has** to change, that's fine. **Call your loan
officer first.** Almost every one of these is survivable with warning and fatal as a
surprise. That's **mistake number four.**

## MODULE 8 — BUILDER VS EXISTING (33:30–36:00) ⏱

**[066]** Honest comparison. Both of these win rows.

Two things people get wrong. **First — builders would rather give you twenty thousand
in incentives than take twenty thousand off the price**, because a price cut sets a
comp that lowers every other house in the subdivision.

So don't negotiate price. **Negotiate everything else — and negotiate hard.** Closing
cost credits. Rate buydowns. Upgrades. Appliances. Landscaping. Fencing. Lot
premium. HOA dues.

**Second, and this bites hard.** Your first-year property tax bill on new
construction may be assessed on an **unimproved lot.** Then the house gets assessed
and your escrow payment jumps — and you get a bill for the shortfall. Both at once.
**Ask, in writing, what the tax basis will be at full assessment.**

**[067]** Client's under contract on a new build. Builder offers **fifteen thousand
dollars toward closing costs — if he uses their preferred lender.**

He calls me and says, look, I like you, but you can't beat fifteen thousand dollars.
And honestly? He might have been right. So we ran it.

Their lender: fifteen thousand credit, rate a quarter point higher, about three
thousand more in Section A. A quarter point was roughly seventy dollars a month. Over
seven years, about fifty-nine hundred. Plus three thousand in fees. So the
fifteen-thousand-dollar credit was really worth about **six thousand one hundred.**

*(beat)*

**And it still won.** Six thousand dollars is six thousand dollars. He used their
lender. **And I told him to.**

*(beat — the important part)*

I'm telling you that story on purpose, because I want you to hear it from someone
with the opposite incentive. **"Don't use the builder's lender" is literally my
business interest.** So hear it from me specifically: **sometimes the builder's
lender is the right answer.**

What is **never** right is taking the incentive without running the comparison. And
the reason builders can offer these at all is that hardly anyone runs it.

Take the builder's Loan Estimate. Get one outside estimate. Compare Section A. Convert
the rate difference into dollars over how long you'll keep the loan. Ten minutes.
That's **mistake number eight.**

And a builder can't stop you from shopping. If anyone implies you're *required* to
use their lender to buy the house, that's worth asking pointed questions about.

Twelve questions in your download kit. Question six is the one they don't expect —
what's the incentive if I *don't* use your lender. Question seven occasionally saves
the most — can I take a price reduction instead. And question nine: **get an
independent inspection on a brand new house.** People skip it because it's new. New
houses are built by humans on deadlines. Inspect it. Twice.

*(AMA BREAK 3 — 45 seconds.)*

Alright. Minute thirty-six. About four minutes left of me talking, and I want to
spend it on the most expensive part of this entire webinar. Not the most
complicated. The most expensive.

## MODULE 9 — THE MISTAKES (36:00–38:30) ⏱

**[068]** Nine mistakes. **You've already met all of them today** — I'm just naming

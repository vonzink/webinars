# Stories — v5

Seven stories. v4 had eight; **one was cut and three were rewritten** because they
were doing a job the audience could feel them doing.

**Disclosure:** composites drawn from real patterns, details changed. Never an
identifiable client. Never imply an outcome is typical or promised.

**Craft rules — every story must have all four:**
1. **A scene**, not a summary. A place, a time of day, an object.
2. **One line of speech.** Somebody says something out loud.
3. **One number.** The number is what survives after the narrative fades.
4. **No explained moral.** The next slide is the moral.

v4's weakest stories each failed rule 1. "Prices went up over six years" is a
claim. A Post-it on a bathroom mirror is a story.

---

## ⭐ Story A — Two Loan Estimates
**Slide 002 · THE COLD OPEN · Number: $6,400**

> *Promoted from minute 24 to minute 0. It was the strongest hook in v4 and it was
> being spent as a mid-deck transition.*

A client sent me two Loan Estimates and asked which one was better. Both thirty-year
fixed. Both the same loan amount. **Both exactly the same interest rate.**

She'd already decided it was a coin flip. She said — "they're identical, right? I'm
just going to pick the one whose office is closer."

Section A on the first one — that's origination charges, the lender's own fees — was
about thirteen hundred dollars.

Section A on the second was about **seven thousand seven hundred.**

**Turn:** *"Nobody defrauded her. Every number was disclosed correctly, on the
government form, exactly as required. The disclosure worked perfectly. The
disclosure only works if somebody reads page two."*
**Teaches:** the entire premise of the webinar.
**Delivery:** the "office is closer" line is what makes her a person instead of an
example. Keep it.

## ⭐ Story B — Priya
**Slide 013 · Number: $105,000**

> *Rewritten. v4 gave her a transmission, a layoff, and a sister in crisis "the same
> spring" — three misfortunes doing conveniently coordinated work. It read written.
> One specific misfortune is more believable than three.*

Priya was approved for five hundred and twenty thousand dollars. Real letter, real
number, she qualified for every dollar of it.

She bought at three ninety.

Two years in, her transmission went. Four thousand dollars, no warning, on a
Thursday. She paid it out of savings and she was annoyed for about a week.

She told me later — and I've repeated this more than anything else anyone has ever
said to me in this job — **"I didn't buy a smaller house. I bought a smaller amount
of fear."**

**Turn:** the industry approves you at the top because guidelines measure whether
you'll *repay*, not whether you'll be *okay*.
**Delivery:** do not moralize. She wasn't timid — she ran the math. If it plays as
"be cautious," it's being delivered wrong.

## ⭐⭐ Story C — The Post-it
**Slide 020 · Number: 6 years · REPLACES v4's Tom & Ashley**

> *v4's version was the weakest thing in the deck: "prices went up" is a claim with
> no scene, no image, and no moment. This is the same lesson with a place to stand.*

Ashley kept the number on a Post-it on the bathroom mirror. **Seventy-one thousand
dollars.** That was twenty percent of the house they wanted, on the street they
wanted, in 2019.

They were serious about it. No vacations that mattered. They tracked it on a
spreadsheet Tom updated every payday.

Four years in they had **fifty-eight thousand dollars** — which is genuinely
extraordinary, and I want to be clear that I admire it.

And one morning Ashley came out of the bathroom and she'd changed the Post-it. Same
house. Same street. New number.

**Ninety-four thousand.**

She told me that was the morning she cried, and that she didn't think it was about
the money. It was that she'd done everything right and the finish line moved anyway.

**Turn:** *"They spent six years chasing a percentage of a number that kept moving —
to avoid a fee that would have come off in five."*

**Required counterweight — do not cut, but say it faster than v4 did:**
> *"And the honest other side: if they'd bought right before a downturn, this story
> reads differently. Waiting isn't always wrong. But it should be a decision, not a
> default. Theirs was a default. Nobody ever told them there was a comparison to
> run."*

**Delivery:** the Post-it is the whole story. Don't rush the moment she changes it.

## ⭐ Story D — "It was only four years"
**Slide 021 · Number: 34 years old**

> *Untouched from v4. It is the most emotionally efficient story in the deck.*

A few years back I'm on a first call with a guy. Good job, decent credit, scraping
together three and a half percent for an FHA loan. Halfway through, completely
offhand, he mentions he did four years in the Army after high school.

I stopped the call. I said — you know you can buy this house with nothing down? And
no monthly mortgage insurance at all?

He didn't. **He was thirty-four. Nobody had ever asked him.**

**Turn:** he almost paid tens of thousands of dollars for the privilege of not
asking a question.
**Delivery:** tell it flat. The flatter you tell it, the harder it hits.

## ⭐ Story E — The color-coded spreadsheet
**Slide 026 · Number: $31,760 · REWRITTEN**

> *v4's version was a fact with a parking lot attached — "he called his brother."
> No person in it. This version makes the emotion the point, and the emotion is the
> webinar's actual theme.*

He had a spreadsheet. Color-coded, tabs across the bottom, the whole thing. He knew
his closing costs to the dollar — **five thousand four hundred** — because he'd read
page one of his Loan Estimate three separate times.

He sat down at the title company. They slid the number across the table.

**Thirty-one thousand seven hundred and sixty dollars.**

And he told me the first thing he felt wasn't panic. **It was embarrassment.** He
was sure he'd missed something obvious that everybody else knew.

He hadn't. He'd read the wrong line — because the wrong line has the more sensible
name.

**Turn:** *"Closing costs, five four hundred. Cash to close, thirty-one seven sixty.
Those are not the same word and they are twenty-six thousand dollars apart."*
**Then — the live callback:** *"Maya and Dev have twenty-eight thousand saved. Go
back and look at that number. They're three thousand seven hundred and sixty dollars
short and they don't know it yet."*
**Delivery:** "It was embarrassment" is the line. It connects to the closing beat of
the whole webinar. Land it.

## ⭐ Story F — The $9,000 sofa
**Slide 042 · Number: 9 days**

> *Untouched. Universally relatable and correctly placed.*

Nine days before closing, a client bought furniture. Nine thousand dollars, zero
percent for twenty-four months, at the store, because it was zero percent and he was
excited — and honestly, who wouldn't be. He was about to have a house with nothing
in it.

Lenders re-pull credit before closing. **Every time.** The new debt hit, his ratios
moved, and his approval — which had already been issued — came apart.

**Turn:** *"He wasn't reckless. He bought a couch for a house he'd already been
approved for. Nobody told him the approval was still alive."*
**Delivery:** genuine sympathy, zero mockery. Half the room was about to do this.

## ⭐⭐ Story G — "I'm not turning down fifteen thousand dollars"
**Slide 044 · Number: $15,000 vs. ~$6,100 — and it still won · REWRITTEN**

> *v4's version was structurally the smartest story in the deck — you argue against
> your own financial interest — and it had no human being in it. It was a math
> problem with a client attached. One line of dialogue fixes it, and the humor buys
> you the credibility for what follows.*

He called me on a Tuesday and I could hear that he'd already decided. He said,
**"I like you. But I'm not turning down fifteen thousand dollars to be loyal
to a guy I met on a webinar."**

Which — fair.

His builder was offering fifteen thousand toward closing costs if he used their
preferred lender. So we ran it. Their rate was a quarter point higher — about
seventy dollars a month, and over the seven years he expected to stay, about
fifty-nine hundred dollars. Plus roughly three thousand more in Section A.

So the fifteen-thousand-dollar credit was really worth about **six thousand one
hundred.**

**And it still won. He used their lender. And I told him to.**

**Turn — the required paragraph, do not cut:**
> *"I'm telling you that story on purpose, because I want you to hear it from
> somebody with the opposite incentive. 'Don't use the builder's lender' is
> literally my business interest. So hear it from me specifically: sometimes the
> builder's lender is the right answer. What's never right is taking the incentive
> without running the comparison — and the reason builders can offer these at all
> is that hardly anybody does."*

## CUT from v4

**The Reyes offer** (five months of looking, lost the house in six hours). It was a
solid story serving a demoted module. It survives as **one line in the mistakes
recap** — *"Mistake one: looking at houses before talking to a lender. I've watched
people lose a house in six hours over this."* — which is all the room it earns at 38
minutes.

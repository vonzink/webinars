## The honest comparison

| Dimension | New construction | Existing |
|---|---|---|
| Price per sq ft | Often higher | Often lower |
| Price negotiation | **Resisted** — protects subdivision comps | **Genuinely negotiable** |
| Incentives | **Substantial and negotiable** | Rare |
| Condition | New, with warranty | Known, inspectable, has a history |
| Timeline | Months; delays are normal | 30–45 days |
| Rate risk | **Long lock or float — real exposure** | Short, predictable |
| Immediate extras | Landscaping, fencing, blinds, appliances, lot premium | Deferred maintenance you inherit |
| Property taxes | **Often land-only at first, then jump** 🔴 | Established, predictable |
| Buyer leverage | Lower — many buyers, one seller | **Higher** — one house, one seller |
| HOA | Usually; often builder-controlled initially | Varies |
| Neighborhood | Unknown; construction continues around you | Visible and knowable |

## ⭐ Why builders give incentives instead of price cuts

A price reduction **sets a comparable sale** that lowers the appraised value of
every remaining home in the subdivision — and can upset buyers who already closed.
Incentives don't show up in the comps the same way.

**The practical implication, and it's the most actionable thing in the module:**
> "Don't negotiate price. Negotiate everything else — and negotiate hard, because
> there is far more room there than people use."

Negotiable in practice: closing cost credits · rate buydowns · upgrades and design
center allowances · appliances · landscaping and fencing · window coverings · lot
premium reduction · HOA dues paid for a period.

## ⭐ The property tax trap 🔴

First-year property tax on new construction may be assessed on an **unimproved
lot.** Once the completed home is assessed, taxes rise — sometimes substantially —
and the escrow account both increases the monthly payment **and** bills for the
shortfall.

**Both hit at once, and it's a genuine budget shock.**

> **Slide 066:** *"Ask, in writing, what the tax basis will be at full assessment.
> Budget from that number, not the first one."*

🔴 Verify current Colorado assessment practice before delivery — assessment rules
and residential assessment rates have been subject to change.

## ⭐ Preferred lender incentives — the analysis

Builder incentives are commonly conditioned on using an affiliated or preferred
lender. **This is legal.** Affiliated business arrangements require disclosure
under RESPA. The issue isn't legality — it's that **hardly anyone runs the
comparison**, which is what makes the offer economical for the builder.

### Story 8's arithmetic
```
Builder credit                      $15,000
Rate 0.25% higher ≈ $70/mo
  × 7 years (expected hold)          $5,880
Extra Section A                      $3,000
Total extra cost                     $8,880
NET VALUE OF INCENTIVE               $6,120   ← builder still wins
```

**He took it. He was told to.**

### The procedure to teach
1. Get the builder's Loan Estimate
2. Get **one** outside Loan Estimate, same day
3. Compare **Section A**
4. Convert the rate difference to dollars **over how long you'll actually keep the
   loan** — the same breakeven skill from Module 5, six minutes earlier
5. Compare that total against the incentive

> "That's it. Ten minutes. And a builder can't stop you from shopping — if anyone
> implies you're *required* to use their lender to buy the house, that's worth
> asking pointed questions about."

**Compliance:** phrase as written. Don't give legal advice about specific contract
terms — direct people to their agent or their own real estate attorney.

## ⭐ The long-lock problem

New construction closing dates move. A lock that expires before closing means
either an extension (**costs money**) or repricing at the current market.

**Questions to ask before signing:**
- What's your delay history on this phase?
- What lock terms are available for my timeline?
- What does an extension cost, and who pays if the delay is the builder's?
- What happens to my earnest money if the build runs past my lock or my lease ends?

## ARTIFACT #7 — 12 Questions to Ask a Builder

1. What exactly is included, and what's an upgrade? In writing.
2. What will my property taxes be at **full assessment**?
3. What's the HOA today, who controls it, and when does control transfer to owners?
4. What's the lot premium, and what am I getting for it?
5. Are landscaping, fencing, window coverings, and appliances included?
6. **What's the incentive if I don't use your preferred lender?**
7. **Can I take a price reduction instead of the incentive?**
8. What's your delay history on this phase, and what happens to my rate lock?
9. Can I get an **independent inspection** before drywall and again before closing?
10. What's the warranty — 1 year, 2 years, 10 years structural — and who honors it?
11. What is planned on the empty land behind this lot?
12. What happens to my earnest money if the build is delayed past my lock or lease?

**Q6** is the one they don't expect. **Q7** occasionally saves the most money.
**Q9** — get an independent inspection on a brand-new house. People skip it because
it's new. New houses are built by humans on deadlines.

## Agent registration — the trap nobody mentions

Many builders require a buyer's agent to be **registered on the buyer's first
visit.** Tour the model home alone and you may lose the ability to bring your own
representation to a transaction where the on-site agent works for the builder.

> **AMA Break 3 seeded question:** *"Can I use my own agent on a new build?"*
> **Answer:** Yes — but register them on your **first visit**.

# Module 5 — Rates, APR & Buydowns

**Time:** 18:00 – 24:30 (6:30) · **Slides:** 040–051
**Job:** Move the audience from "get the lowest rate" to "get the lowest total
cost for how long I'll actually keep this loan." This is the module that most
separates this webinar from every other one.
**Artifact:** The Rate Rule — half a point ≈ $140/mo ≈ $23,000 of house
**Demos:** APR Calculator (047), Amortization Calculator (051)

---

## The intellectual arc

1. Rates aren't a single number set by the Fed — they're a market, and *your* rate
   is a market rate plus adjustments for your file. (041–042)
2. Rate differences translate into a concrete, memorizable amount of house. (043)
3. APR exists because rate alone can't be compared — and APR is itself flawed. (044–047)
4. Points are a bet with a computable breakeven. (048–049)
5. Temporary buydowns are a different instrument entirely. (050)
6. And here's where every dollar of it actually goes. (051)

## Beat sheet

### 18:05 — Slide 041 — What actually moves mortgage rates

> "Let's kill the biggest one first. **The Federal Reserve does not set mortgage
> rates.** The Fed sets the federal funds rate — an overnight bank-to-bank rate.
> It influences short-term borrowing: credit cards, HELOCs, auto loans."

> "Thirty-year mortgage rates track the **bond market** — specifically mortgage-
> backed securities, which move roughly with the 10-year Treasury. Here's the
> proof you can check yourself: there have been Fed cuts where mortgage rates went
> *up* the same day. If the Fed set them, that couldn't happen."

What actually moves them:
- Investor demand for mortgage bonds
- Inflation expectations — inflation eats a fixed return, so investors demand more
- Employment and growth data
- The spread between MBS and Treasuries (widens when markets get nervous)

> "And the honest conclusion: **nobody knows where rates are going.** Not me, not
> your agent, not the guy on the podcast. Every forecast you've seen for the last
> several years has been revised. So any strategy that requires you to be right
> about the direction of rates is not a strategy. It's a wager."

### 18:45 — Slide 042 — The 7 things that change *your* rate

The market sets a base. Your file adjusts it:

1. **Credit score** — the largest single lever, and it moves in tiers, not smoothly
2. **Loan-to-value** — your down payment
3. **Loan program** — conventional vs. FHA vs. VA vs. USDA price differently
4. **Occupancy** — primary vs. second home vs. investment (big jumps)
5. **Property type** — single-family vs. condo vs. multi-unit
6. **Loan term** — 15-year prices below 30-year
7. **Points** — whether you're paying to buy the rate down

> "Which is why 'what's your rate today' is an unanswerable question. Not evasive —
> unanswerable. It's like asking a doctor 'what's the dose.' Dose of what, for
> whom? Anyone who fires back a number without knowing five of those seven things
> is quoting you a rate you may not qualify for. That's not a quote. That's bait."

### 19:20 — Slide 043 — ARTIFACT #4: The Rate Rule

Maya & Dev's $427,500 loan:

| Rate | P&I |
|---|---|
| 5.500% | $2,427 |
| 6.000% | $2,563 |
| **6.500%** | **$2,702** |
| 7.000% | $2,844 |
| 7.500% | $2,990 |

> "Memorize this shape, because it's portable. **On a loan around this size, half
> a percent of rate is roughly $140 a month — and $140 a month is roughly $23,000
> of purchase price.**"

> "That's the useful conversion. When rates move half a point, the question isn't
> 'should I panic.' It's: *do I still want this house at $23,000 more, or do I
> want a house that costs $23,000 less?* That's a decision you can actually make.
> 'Rates went up' is not."

### 19:55 — Slide 044 — APR isn't your interest rate

> "Two numbers on every mortgage disclosure. They are never the same, and almost
> nobody can tell you why."

- **Interest rate** — what your payment is calculated from
- **APR** — the rate *plus* certain lender costs, spread across the full loan term,
  expressed as a yearly rate

> "The analogy: the **rate is the sticker price on the car. The APR is closer to
> the out-the-door price.** Two dealers can advertise the same sticker and hand you
> very different final numbers, because one of them has $6,000 of fees stapled to
> the back page."

> "APR exists because lenders figured out you'd shop on rate alone — so they'd
> advertise a beautiful rate and make it up in fees. APR was the regulatory
> response. It's imperfect. It's still the best single comparison number you have."

### 20:25 — Slide 045 — Two loans, side by side

Same house. Same $427,500. Two lenders.

| | **Loan A** | **Loan B** |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | 6.750% | **6.250%** |
| Discount points | 0 | 2.00 = **$8,550** |
| Other lender fees | $1,400 | $1,400 |
| **Monthly P&I** | $2,773 | **$2,632** |

> "Which one's better?"

*(Pause. Let chat answer. They will say B.)*

> "Almost everyone says B. B has the lower rate and the lower payment — both
> things you've been trained to shop for. And B *might* be better. But you cannot
> tell yet, because nobody has told you the only thing that decides it."

### 21:00 — Slide 046 — When APR misleads `[CUT-1]`

APR assumes you keep the loan for the **entire term**. Three places that breaks:

1. **You won't keep it 30 years.** Most people move or refinance far sooner. APR
   spreads upfront costs across 360 months you won't use.
2. **ARMs** — APR has to guess at future adjustments using today's index.
3. **Lenders don't all include identical items**, and third-party costs you can
   shop for muddy the comparison.

> "So: use APR as a **flag**, not a verdict. Big gap between rate and APR means
> big upfront costs — go find them. That's what it's for."

### 22:05 — Slide 048 — What you're actually buying with points

> "One 'point' is 1% of the loan amount, paid at closing, to permanently lower
> your rate. On $427,500, one point is $4,275."

> "It is prepaid interest. You are handing over cash today to reduce every payment
> for as long as you keep the loan. Like buying a **gym membership for the year
> instead of paying monthly** — cheaper per month, and a total waste if you move
> in March."

Also name the opposite: **lender credits.** Take a *higher* rate and the lender
pays some of your closing costs.

> "That's the trade running in reverse, and it's frequently the right move for a
> first-time buyer who is cash-tight and equity-rich in nothing. Cash today can be
> worth more to you than a slightly lower payment. Nobody offers this. Ask for it."

### 22:35 — Slide 049 — Breakeven: the only question that matters

**$8,550 ÷ $141/month = 60.6 months ≈ 5 years 1 month.**

> "Sixty-one months. That's the whole analysis. If Maya and Dev keep this exact
> loan longer than five years and one month, the points won. Shorter, and they
> lit $8,550 on fire for a lower number on a piece of paper."

> "So the real question — the one nobody asks you — is: **how long will you keep
> this specific loan?** Not this house. This loan. Because refinancing ends the
> loan even if you never move."

> "First-time buyers systematically overestimate this. You think thirty years. The
> real distribution is much shorter — people move, get married, have kids, take
> jobs, refinance. If your honest answer is 'I don't know,' that's not a failure —
> it's information, and it argues against paying points."

**PORTABLE RULE:** *Points are a bet on how long you keep this exact loan. Compute
the breakeven in months. If you won't clearly pass it, don't.*

### 23:10 — Slide 050 — Temporary buydowns `[CUT-4]`

Different instrument. Not the same as points.

**2-1 buydown:** year 1 your rate acts 2% lower, year 2 acts 1% lower, year 3
onward it's the real rate. The difference is prefunded into an escrow account —
usually **paid by the seller or the builder as a concession.**

> "The good: real cash-flow relief in the two most expensive years of ownership,
> often paid by somebody else. If unused funds remain — you refinance or sell —
> they're generally credited toward your loan rather than lost."

> "The trap: **you qualify at the real rate, not the bought-down rate.** You will
> be paying the full payment in year three. If the plan depends on refinancing
> before then, that plan depends on rates you cannot control."

> "And the sharper version — when a builder offers this, ask what it's costing
> you. Sometimes a rate buydown is funded by a price that was set high enough to
> pay for it. A **price reduction** is permanent. A temporary buydown is two
> years. Ask which one you can have. Sometimes you can have either."

### 23:45 — Slide 051 — DEMO 3: Amortization

Run-book: `resources/amortization-demo.md`. Fallback: 051-fallback.

Maya & Dev, payment one of 360:

> "$2,702. Of that, **$2,315 is interest and $387 is principal.** Eighty-three
> percent of your first payment is rent on money."

> "It does not flip quickly. On this loan, the month where principal finally
> exceeds interest is **month 218.** Year eighteen."

> "Total interest over 30 years: about **$545,000** on a $427,500 loan."

*(Let that sit. Don't rush it. This is the most-remembered number in the webinar.)*

> "Now here's the lever, and it's the reason I don't mind showing you the scary
> number."

**THE $100 EXPERIMENT** — add $100/month from day one:
- Paid off in **~27 years** instead of 30
- Total interest **~$480,900** instead of ~$545,200
- **Saves ~$64,300 and about 3 years**

> "One hundred dollars. Sixty-four thousand back. And unlike almost everything else
> today, this one is entirely under your control — no lender, no approval, no
> market. Every extra dollar you send early kills interest that would have
> compounded for decades."

> "Two cautions. Confirm the payment is applied to **principal** — some servicers
> apply extra to the next payment instead, which does nothing. And don't do this
> before you have an emergency fund. Money in the house is hard to get back out."

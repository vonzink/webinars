# Module 3 (file 04) — What Can You Actually Afford

**Time:** 7:00 – 13:00 (6:00) · **Slides:** 017–029
**Delivery position:** THIRD. Taught *before* programs.
**Job:** Separate "what you're approved for" from "what you can live with" — and
introduce the canonical example that carries the rest of the webinar.
**Artifact:** The Payment-Shock Test
**Demo:** Budget Calculator (slide 027)

---

## Why this precedes programs

The original topic order put programs before budgeting. That's backwards. If you
tell someone they can buy with 3% down before they know what the payment does to
their life, you've handed them a way to get into trouble faster. Affordability
first, then access. This ordering is a deliberate, defensible ethics choice and
should be preserved.

This module also **introduces Maya & Dev and the $450,000 house** (slide 022).
Every subsequent module builds on these exact numbers. See
`references/canonical-example.md`.

## Beat sheet

### 7:05 — Slide 018 — Story 2: $520,000 / $390,000

> "Priya came to me pre-approved for $520,000. Real number, real letter, she
> qualified for every dollar of it. She bought at $390,000."

> "I want to be careful here, because I'm not telling you she was scared or that
> she settled. She ran the math and decided she'd rather have $900 a month than
> a fourth bedroom."

> "Two years later her car needed a transmission, her company did a layoff round
> she survived but didn't enjoy, and her sister needed help. All in the same
> spring. She handled all three and never missed a beat."

> "She told me something I've repeated ever since. She said: **'I didn't buy a
> smaller house. I bought a smaller amount of fear.'**"

> "Here's the uncomfortable part, and I'm including myself in this. The mortgage
> industry approves you for the top of your range. That's not a conspiracy —
> it's just that our guidelines measure whether you'll *repay*, not whether
> you'll be *okay*. Those are different questions. Only one of them is being
> asked on your behalf, and it isn't the second one. So you have to ask it."

### 7:50 — Slide 019 — EXERCISE 1: "The Number"

> "Everybody, right now. Piece of paper, phone notes, back of an envelope.
> Write down the highest monthly housing payment you could pay **every single
> month for the next ten years** and still take the trip, still fix the car,
> still say yes to dinner."

> "Don't compute anything. Don't look anything up. Gut number. Write it down and
> hold onto it — we're coming back to it in five minutes."

*(15 seconds of silence on the timer. Let it be awkward. The silence is the
exercise.)*

### 8:20 — Slide 020 — Two budgets

| | The bank's budget | Your budget |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | "Will you repay?" | "Will you be okay?" |
| Looks at | Gross income, documented debts | Take-home, real life |
| Ignores | Childcare, groceries, retirement, travel, pets, the dog's surgery | Nothing |
| Output | Your **maximum** | Your **comfortable** |

> "One of these is on your pre-approval letter. The other one is your actual life.
> Nobody in the transaction is going to compute the second one for you. Today
> you're going to."

### 8:50 — Slide 021 — Anatomy: P-I-T-I

- **P**rincipal — the part that actually buys the house
- **I**nterest — rent on the money
- **T**axes — property taxes, collected monthly, paid by the lender for you
- **I**nsurance — homeowners insurance, same arrangement

> "That last arrangement is called **escrow**, and it's the most misunderstood
> thing in this business. Escrow is a subscription your lender runs on your
> behalf. They collect a twelfth of your taxes and insurance every month, hold it,
> and pay the bills when they're due. It is not a fee. It's not their money. And
> your payment will change when the tax bill changes — usually up, usually once a
> year, usually as a surprise. Now it won't be."

### 9:25 — Slide 022 — The full stack — **MEET MAYA & DEV** 🔑

> "Let's stop being abstract. For the rest of this webinar we're buying one house.
> Same house, all forty minutes. Everything I teach you, we're going to apply to
> this exact loan so you can watch it accumulate."

**$450,000 · 5% down ($22,500) · $427,500 loan · 30-year fixed · 6.500% hypothetical**

| | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Principal & Interest | $2,702 |
| Taxes | $206 |
| Insurance | $150 |
| Mortgage Insurance | $125 |
| HOA | $0 |
| **Total** | **$3,183** |

> "$3,183. Say that number out loud once, because we're going to see it about
> eleven more times."

> "And if this house had an HOA — a lot of Colorado condos and newer
> neighborhoods do — add $250 and it's $3,433. HOA dues count against you exactly
> like a mortgage payment does, and they can be raised without your vote."

### 10:00 — Slide 023 — The hidden five

Nobody's calculator includes these:

1. **Maintenance reserve** — plan 1–2% of value per year. On $450k that's
   $375–$750/month, amortized. It won't be smooth: nothing for two years, then a
   $14,000 roof.
2. **The utility delta** — a house is not an apartment. Budget +$100–200.
3. **Furnishing** — the empty-house tax. It's real, it's $3,000–10,000, and it's
   the #1 reason people put furniture on credit in month one.
4. **Commute change** — cheaper house, farther out, +$200/mo in gas and time.
5. **Escalation** — taxes and insurance go up. Insurance in Colorado has gone up
   meaningfully in recent years (wildfire and hail exposure). Assume increases.

> "Planning figure for today: **+$450/month.** So Maya and Dev's real number isn't
> $3,183. It's about **$3,633**. They're renting at $2,150. That's not a $1,033
> change. It's a **$1,483** change. That's the number that decides whether this is
> a good year or a bad one."

### 10:35 — Slide 024 — DTI

> "**Debt-to-income.** It's the single number that decides how much house you get,
> and almost nobody outside this business can define it."

> "Take everything you're contractually obligated to pay every month. Divide by
> your gross monthly income — before taxes. That percentage is your DTI."

> "Think of it like a **weight limit on a suitcase.** The airline doesn't care
> what's in the bag. It cares what it weighs. Underwriting doesn't care whether
> that $500 is a car you love or a car you regret. It's $500."

### 11:05 — Slide 025 — Front-end and back-end `[EXP-1: walk the arithmetic]`

Maya & Dev: $9,500 gross/month · $780 other debt

- **Front-end** (housing only): $3,183 ÷ $9,500 = **33.5%**
- **Back-end** (housing + debts): $3,963 ÷ $9,500 = **41.7%**

> "Back-end is the one that matters. Conventional loans commonly go to about 45%,
> and with strong automated-underwriting findings can stretch to 50%. FHA can go
> higher with compensating factors. VA doesn't use a hard DTI cap the same way —
> it uses **residual income**, which is honestly a smarter test: how much cash is
> left over after everything, given your family size and where you live."

> "But watch this — and this is the whole point of the module. At 41.7%, Maya and
> Dev are comfortably approvable. **They could go to 45% and buy about $60,000
> more house.** Their DTI would still say yes. Their life would say something
> else. The ratio has no opinion about their life."

### 11:30 — Slide 026 — What counts `[CUT-3]`

**Counts as income** (with documentation and, usually, a 2-year history):
base salary · hourly at average hours · overtime, bonus, commission (2-yr average) ·
self-employment net income after write-offs · documented rental income ·
child support/alimony if continuing 3+ years · certain retirement and disability

**Doesn't count:** cash tips you don't report · a raise you've been promised ·
a side gig with no history · money your parents "will help with" ·
income from a job you start next month (with narrow exceptions)

**Counts as debt:** car loans · student loans (even deferred — a percentage or
IBR payment is used, and the rule varies by program) · credit card minimums ·
personal loans · child support/alimony paid · co-signed loans **you don't pay**

**Doesn't count:** utilities · cell phone · insurance · groceries · streaming ·
childcare *(and this is the one that hurts — daycare can be $1,500/month and
underwriting will not see a dollar of it. Your budget must.)*

> "Big one: **write-offs.** If you're self-employed and you had a great year on
> paper but wrote it all down to nothing for taxes, we qualify you on the
> nothing. Talk to a lender the year *before* you buy, not the March after."

### 12:00 — Slide 027 — DEMO 1: Budget Calculator

Run-book: `resources/budget-demo.md`. Fallback slide: 027-fallback.

> "Every calculator online does this forwards: here's a price, here's a payment.
> Mine does it backwards, because backwards is the direction you actually think."

Live: enter **the poll's most common comfortable payment**, and show the price it
supports. Then toggle the hidden five on, and watch the price fall.

> "Look at that. Same comfort level, $60,000 less house — and that's the honest
> version. Every calculator that leaves that off is quietly telling you that you
> can afford more than you can."

### 12:40 — Slide 028 — POLL 2

**"What monthly housing payment feels genuinely comfortable to you?"**
Ranges. Then: *"Now compare it to the number you wrote down at minute eight. Same?
Higher? For most people the written one is lower — that's your gut, and your gut
did that before it knew what houses cost."*

### 12:50 — Slide 029 — Artifact #2 + AMA BREAK 1

**The Payment-Shock Test:**
> "For three months before you buy: every month, transfer the difference between
> your rent and your projected all-in payment into a separate savings account.
> For Maya and Dev that's $1,483 a month."
>
> "Two things happen. One, you find out whether it's actually livable — and you
> find out for free instead of finding out in month four of a thirty-year loan.
> Two, at the end you have $4,449 toward your closing costs. This test pays you
> to take it."

**AMA Break 1 (45 seconds)** — seeded question if chat is quiet:
*"What if my income isn't the same every month?"*
